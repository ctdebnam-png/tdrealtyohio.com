How to look up zoning on a Columbus or Westerville property before you sell or lease it

Zoning decides what a buyer or tenant can legally do with a building or a lot. If you own commercial property in Columbus or Westerville, you can check it yourself in a few minutes with free city maps. Here is where to look, what the results mean, and who to ask when the answer isn't what you hoped.

Why zoning comes up in almost every commercial deal

A buyer looking at a small retail building wants to know whether their use is allowed there. A tenant signing a lease for a shop or office has the same question. If the answer turns out to be no after an offer or a lease is signed, the deal can stall while someone applies for a variance or a rezoning, or it can fall apart. Owners who know their zoning before they list or advertise a space can describe the property accurately and point serious buyers and tenants to the right uses from the start.

None of the steps below cost anything. They take an address or a parcel number and a little patience with city websites.

Step 1: Confirm which city's rules apply

Before you open any map, make sure you know which government zones the property. A mailing address is not proof. Central Ohio has many addresses that carry a city's name but sit in a township or a neighboring city, and each one has its own zoning code and its own boards. The county auditor's parcel record shows the taxing district, and the city maps below only cover land inside that city. If the parcel doesn't show up on a city's map, that's a sign to check with the township or the other city instead.

Step 2 in Columbus: the Online Zoning Map

The City of Columbus Department of Building and Zoning Services publishes an Online Zoning Map at gis.columbus.gov/zoning. It shows base zoning, area commissions, historic districts and commercial overlays. You can search by address or by parcel, and you can create a PDF report for a point on the map, which is handy to keep in your listing file. [1]

Each of those layers matters for a different reason:

  • Base zoning is the district itself, which sets the allowed uses and the basic building standards.
  • Area commissions are neighborhood groups that the map shows by boundary. If your property sits in one, expect it to be part of the conversation on a rezoning or variance request.
  • Historic districts can add review for exterior changes.
  • Commercial overlays add standards on top of the base district along certain corridors.

Columbus has two zoning codes right now

This is the part that trips people up. Columbus adopted a new zoning code in 2024, found in Title 34 of the Columbus City Codes, and is putting it in place in phases through what the city calls the Zone In Initiative. Parcels that haven't been rezoned to a 2024 code district are still governed by the older code in Title 33. [3]

So when you read a Columbus zoning designation, check which code it comes from before you look up what it allows. Some Title 33 chapters still apply to parcels under the 2024 code, including the chapter on the Board of Zoning Adjustment, appeals and variances (Chapter 3307) and the chapter on amendments and rezoning (Chapter 3310). Others, such as the old residential, apartment, C-1, C-3 and manufacturing district chapters, do not. [3] The full text of the City Code is published online through Municode. [2]

Variances and rezonings in Columbus

A variance asks permission to depart from a standard, like a setback or a parking count, while keeping the same district. A rezoning changes the district. They follow different paths. Variances and appeals fall under Chapter 3307 and the Board of Zoning Adjustment. Rezonings are amendments to the zoning map, handled under Chapter 3310, and they become legislation that you can follow on Columbus City Council's online agendas and legislation search. [2] [3]

It also helps to read the area plan for the neighborhood. The city's Planning Documents Library holds them, and they show what the city has said it wants to see in that area. [2]

For questions about a specific parcel, Building and Zoning Services lists zoninginfo@columbus.gov for zoning information and recordscenter@columbus.gov for research and records requests. [2] The office is at 111 North Front Street, Columbus, phone (614) 645-7433, open Monday through Friday, 9:00 am to 4:00 pm. The Zoning Review room closes from 9:00 to 10:30 am on the second Tuesday of each month for staff training. [1]

Step 2 in Westerville: the zoning map and code

The City of Westerville posts both a static PDF zoning map and an interactive zoning map on its Planning and Zoning documents page. Its zoning code is Part Eleven and a portion of Part Twelve of the city's codified ordinances, which are hosted by American Legal Publishing and linked from the same page. [5]

Westerville also has overlay districts that can apply on top of the base zoning: the Olde Westerville, Central College, Hoover Gateway and Source Water Protection special overlay districts, plus a South State Street Corridor overlay with its own page. [5] If your property is in or near Uptown or along one of those corridors, check the overlay before you assume the base district tells the whole story.

Who decides in Westerville

Westerville's Planning and Zoning staff support three city boards. The Planning Commission reviews new development, projects and uses. The Board of Zoning Appeals hears requests to vary from the standards of the zoning code, such as setbacks. The third is the Uptown Review Board. [4] If a buyer's plan needs one of these boards, ask the city's Planning and Zoning staff early how the process works and what it costs, since the city's pages we checked don't list fees or timelines.

What to have ready before you list or lease

For a commercial sale or lease, a short zoning packet saves time for everyone:

  • The parcel number and the jurisdiction that zones it.
  • The zoning map printout or PDF report showing the district and any overlays.
  • For Columbus, a note on whether the parcel is under the 2024 code or Title 33.
  • Any past variances or rezonings you know about for the property.

A map lookup tells you the district. It doesn't tell you whether a particular buyer's use is permitted, needs a conditional approval, or needs a variance. That's a question for the city's zoning staff or a land use attorney, and a buyer or tenant will often confirm it themselves during due diligence.
