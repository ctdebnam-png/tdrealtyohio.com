Ohio security deposit rules for landlords: the 30-day notice, interest, and what you can deduct

If you own a rental house, a duplex or a small apartment building in Columbus, Westerville or anywhere else in Ohio, the security deposit is where small mistakes get expensive. Ohio's rules are short and specific. The core of them sits in one section of the Revised Code, and getting them wrong can cost you the withheld amount a second time plus the tenant's attorney fees. Here's what the law says, with the public source for each point.

Which rentals these rules cover

The rules below come from Ohio Revised Code Chapter 5321, the landlord-tenant law for residential premises. The chapter defines a security deposit as any deposit of money or property that secures the tenant's performance under a rental agreement, and it counts oral agreements as rental agreements, not just written leases. [1] It doesn't cover hotels and other transient stays, among other exceptions listed in the same section. [1] Commercial leases (an office, a shop, a warehouse) aren't residential premises, so they're governed by the lease itself and general contract law instead.

The definition of landlord is broad too. It includes the owner, the owner's agent, and anyone the owner authorizes to manage the property or collect rent. [1] If a property manager holds the deposit, the rules still apply.

Interest on larger deposits

Many landlords don't know this one. Under Ohio Revised Code 5321.16(A), the part of a deposit above $50 or one month's rent, whichever is greater, earns interest at 5% a year if the tenant stays six months or more. The landlord computes it and pays it to the tenant every year. [2]

A quick example. Rent is $1,500 a month and the deposit is $2,000. The excess over one month's rent is $500. If the tenant stays at least six months, the landlord owes 5% of $500 a year, or $25. If the deposit equals one month's rent or less, no interest is owed under this section.

A deposit of one month's rent or less avoids the interest calculation entirely. If you ask for more, put a reminder on your calendar to pay the interest each year.

What you can take out of the deposit

When the rental agreement ends, the landlord may apply the deposit to two things: past due rent, and damages the landlord suffered because the tenant didn't meet the tenant's duties under Ohio Revised Code 5321.05 or the rental agreement. [2]

Section 5321.05 lists those duties. Among them, the tenant must keep the part of the premises they use safe and sanitary, use the electrical and plumbing fixtures properly, and not destroy, deface, damage or remove any fixture, appliance or other part of the premises, whether on purpose or through negligence. [3] A deduction should tie back to one of these duties or to a specific term in the lease.

Good records make the deduction easy to defend. Take dated photos or video at move-in and move-out, use a move-in condition checklist signed by both sides, and keep receipts and invoices for any repair you charge.

The 30-day written notice

This is the rule that matters most. Any deduction must be itemized and identified in a written notice delivered to the tenant, together with the amount due, within 30 days after the rental agreement ends and the tenant gives back possession. [2]

Put plainly: within 30 days you send a letter listing each deduction and its amount, plus a check for whatever's left. If nothing is deducted, the full deposit still goes back within that window.

The tenant has a job here too. The statute says the tenant shall give the landlord a forwarding or new address in writing. If the tenant doesn't, the tenant isn't entitled to the double damages or attorney fees described below. [2] It's worth asking for that address in writing at move-out, and keeping a copy.

What happens if the landlord gets it wrong

If the landlord doesn't follow the 30-day notice rule, the tenant may recover the money due, plus damages equal to the amount wrongfully withheld, plus reasonable attorney fees. [2] A $1,200 deposit that should have come back can turn into $2,400 plus the tenant's legal bill.

A lease can't write around this. Ohio Revised Code 5321.13 says no provision of the chapter may be modified or waived by an oral or written agreement, except for one narrow exception about the landlord taking on tenant duties. [4]

Two related rules worth knowing

Name and address in the lease. Every written residential rental agreement must include the name and address of the owner and of the owner's agent, if there is one. With an oral agreement, the landlord must give the tenant that information in writing at the start of the occupancy. [5] If the owner is an LLC or other entity, the statute spells out which address to use. [5]

Entering the unit. Except in an emergency or where it's impracticable, the landlord must give reasonable notice before entering and enter only at reasonable times. The statute presumes 24 hours is reasonable notice unless there's evidence to the contrary. [6] This comes up often at move-out, when you're showing the unit to the next tenant or a buyer. The tenant can't unreasonably refuse access for those showings. [3]

If you're buying or selling a rental property

When a rental sells with tenants in place, the deposits have to be dealt with at closing. A buyer of an occupied rental should ask for a rent roll that lists each unit's deposit, copies of the leases, and how the deposits will be credited at closing. A seller should have that list ready before the property goes on the market. Ask your title company or attorney how the deposits and any interest owed will be handled in the purchase contract.

A short checklist

  • Decide the deposit amount knowing that the excess over one month's rent earns 5% a year after six months.
  • Put the owner's name and address, and the agent's if any, in the lease.
  • Document condition at move-in and move-out with dated photos.
  • Ask for the tenant's forwarding address in writing.
  • Within 30 days, send the itemized notice and the balance.
  • Keep copies of everything you sent and when.
