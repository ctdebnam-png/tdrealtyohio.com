New 2026 property values in Franklin and Delaware counties: how to check yours and when you can challenge it

Both Franklin and Delaware counties updated property values in 2026. If you own a home, a rental or a commercial building in either county, your auditor's value for 2026 has probably changed, and that value feeds the tax bills that arrive next year. The informal review windows have closed. The formal route, a complaint to the county Board of Revision, is still open into March 2027. Here's how the process works, with the public sources for each step.

Why values changed this year

Ohio law requires each county auditor to appraise every parcel at its true value in money at least once every six years. [1] In the third year after that six-year reappraisal, the county does an update. [2] 2026 is an update year for both Franklin and Delaware counties. Franklin County's auditor calls it a state-required update that "updates values for every property in the county." [3] Delaware County's auditor runs its own 2026 Triennial Valuation Update. [4]

The state also reset the schedule this year. In January 2026 the Ohio Department of Taxation moved sixteen counties back one year, Delaware and Franklin among them, and both now have their next full reappraisal in 2030. [5]

Step 1: Look up your 2026 value

Franklin County set up a separate site for the update, called 2026 Know Your Home Value, at audr-kyhv.franklincountyohio.gov. Tentative values went online and were mailed in June 2026, and the auditor said values will be finalized in December. [3]

In Delaware County, the auditor's 2026 Triennial Valuation Update page links to a "Check your Proposed Value" lookup, and the county's GIS property search shows each parcel's record. [4]

When you look at the number, keep one thing in mind. The value the auditor shows is not your tax. Taxes are figured on a percentage of that value, and Ohio law caps it at 35% of true value. [6] Levies and credits then apply. Franklin County's auditor puts it plainly: a change in appraised value "does not result in an equal percentage change in property taxes." [7]

Step 2: The informal reviews are over

Each county offered an informal review before values were final. Franklin County held Property Value Review sessions over the summer, about 20 minutes each, virtual or in person, running through September 5. [7] Delaware County says its informal review period has ended too. [4] If you missed those windows, the formal complaint is what's left for tax year 2026.

Step 3: File a complaint with the Board of Revision if you still disagree

Ohio Revised Code 5715.19 lets an owner challenge a parcel's value by filing a complaint with the county Board of Revision on or before March 31 of the following tax year. [8] For tax year 2026 that means March 31, 2027.

  • Franklin County: the auditor's mediation page says this filing season opened August 25, 2026, and closes March 31, 2027. [9]
  • Delaware County: the auditor says a complaint can be filed with the Delaware County Board of Revision beginning January 1, 2027, and no later than March 31, 2027. The form is on the auditor's forms page. [4]

The owner can file, and so can certain people the owner hires. The statute names, among others, a real estate broker licensed under Ohio Revised Code Chapter 4735 who is retained by the owner. [8] Many owners file on their own.

One limit matters for planning. Under the same section, you generally can't file again for the same parcel in the same three-year period unless something new happened after the prior tax lien date, such as an arm's-length sale, a casualty loss, a substantial improvement, or for some properties a big change in occupancy. [8] If you file now, make it your best case.

What makes a complaint stronger

The Board of Revision is deciding what the property was worth as of the tax lien date. Opinions about taxes being too high don't move that number. Evidence does. Franklin County's guidance for its review sessions listed the kinds of documents that help: recent sales of comparable properties, a recent professional appraisal, photos, and records of significant repairs. [7] The same kinds of evidence are the backbone of a formal complaint.

A recent sale of the property itself is often the strongest evidence of value, as long as it was an arm's-length sale between unrelated parties. If you bought in the last year or two, have your closing statement ready.

For a rental or commercial building, the case usually turns on what the building actually earns and what similar buildings have sold for. Have rent rolls, leases, vacancy history and operating expenses ready, along with any appraisal done for financing.

Franklin County's mediation option

Franklin County offers mediation as a way to settle a Board of Revision case without a full hearing. A neutral mediator works with the parties by phone, usually for 20 to 30 minutes, and the Board must approve any settlement. The county says that typically, a case asking for less than a $50,000 reduction in value would qualify. The Board of Revision decides which path a case takes. [9]

If you're buying or selling right now

Sellers: the 2026 value may be what a buyer sees first on the auditor's site, and the tax figure in your listing may be based on the old value. Check both before you list so the numbers you give buyers are current.

Buyers: the tax amount on a listing reflects the bill the seller paid. After an update year, next year's bill can be different. Look up the parcel's 2026 value on the county site, and ask your agent or the title company how taxes will be prorated at closing. Our post on the Ohio conveyance fee covers another closing cost that's tied to the sale price.

Commercial owners thinking about selling: a fresh value and a clear record of what the building earns help in both a tax appeal and a sale. Our checklist for selling commercial land in central Ohio covers the other items buyers will ask about.

A short checklist

  • Look up your 2026 value on the Franklin County Know Your Home Value site or the Delaware County auditor's site.
  • Compare it with recent sales of similar properties near you.
  • If you still disagree, file a Board of Revision complaint by March 31, 2027.
  • Attach your evidence: comparable sales, an appraisal, photos, repair records, and for income property, rents and expenses.
  • Remember the once-per-period limit before you file.
