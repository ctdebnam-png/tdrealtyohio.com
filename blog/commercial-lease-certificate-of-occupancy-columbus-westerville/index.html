Leasing commercial space in Columbus or Westerville? Check the certificate of occupancy first

You found a tenant for your empty storefront or office suite, and they want to sign. Before they do, there's a document worth pulling: the certificate of occupancy. In Ohio, a building can't legally be occupied without one, and a tenant who plans to use the space differently from the last occupant may need new plans, permits and inspections before they open. Finding that out after the lease is signed can push back the start date, and often the rent with it. Here's how the rules work, with the public source for each point.

What a certificate of occupancy is

The Ohio Building Code is the starting point. As the Ohio Department of Commerce's certificate of occupancy application puts it, Section 111.1 of the Ohio Building Code says no building or structure, in whole or in part, shall be used or occupied until the building official has issued a certificate of occupancy. [1]

Columbus Building and Zoning Services defines the certificate as the final document issued when establishing or changing the use, showing that the structure has passed all inspections required to legally occupy it. [2] The key words are "changing the use." A CO isn't tied to one tenant. It's tied to how the space is used.

When a new tenant triggers a new CO

The state form asks for the current use group and the proposed use group when part of a building changes from one occupancy to another, so the code treats a different kind of use as a different occupancy. [1] A tenant who will use the space the way the last one did is a simpler case than one who won't.

Columbus calls this a change of occupancy: a change in the purpose or level of activity within a structure that requires a submittal, review and approval of adjusted building and site plans, plus inspections of the work done to make the change. [2] If a tenant wants to turn a retail bay into a restaurant, for example, expect the city to look at whether that's a change of occupancy. The state treats it the same way. When plans are filed for an alteration, an addition or a change of occupancy, the certificate of occupancy is issued once all required inspections are complete. [1]

Interior work counts too. Columbus defines an alteration as any change to an existing building that requires one or more inspections. [2] A tenant build-out with new walls, plumbing or electrical work runs through the permit process even when the use stays the same, and the inspections at the end are what clear the space for occupancy.

Zoning is a separate check

The building code asks whether the space is safe for the use. Zoning asks whether the use is allowed on that lot at all. In Columbus, a certificate of zoning clearance is required before the establishment, change or modification in the use of any building, structure or land. [2] The city's zoning definition of change of use notes that it may bring the need for more parking or loading. [2] That matters for a restaurant or a medical office going into a strip center that was parked for retail.

Our post on how to look up zoning in Columbus and Westerville walks through the city maps and how to confirm which code applies to a parcel.

Finding the existing certificate in Columbus

Start with what's already on file. If you can't find your copy, Columbus has a published procedure for getting a certificate of occupancy for an existing structure. You submit a building permit application through the city's Citizen Access Portal, or on paper with two copies of the floor plan. [3] The plan shows the use group, construction type, room names, occupant load, exits, exit signs and emergency lighting, and fire alarm and suppression status. [3] The fee is on the city's current fee schedule. [3]

Two conditions in that procedure are worth knowing before you market the space. You have to show the occupancy has existed before; if it hasn't, a building permit for a change of occupancy is required instead. And all pending violations or orders of the building official have to be resolved first. [3] If an old order is sitting on the building, you want to know now, not when your tenant's contractor pulls permits.

Columbus posts its building permit application, zoning review application and related forms on the Building and Zoning Services documents and forms page. [4]

How it works in Westerville

Westerville's building division says that before any work starts, the owner or an authorized agent submits construction plans and an application, and that a certificate of occupancy or final approval is given to the permit holder once the work and the required inspections are done. [5] The city runs two online systems: a Permit Portal for simpler permits and a Plan Review Portal for a new commercial building, a major renovation or site development. [6] Commercial permits can only be issued to licensed contractors. [6] For a specific suite, the city's permit staff are the people to ask whether a tenant's plans need review; the contact is on the forms and portals page. [6]

Westerville also has overlay districts that can add rules on top of the base zoning. The zoning post linked above covers them.

What this means for the lease

None of this is a reason to pass on a good tenant. It's a reason to sort out a few things before the lease is final:

  • Pull the current certificate of occupancy and the last use of the space.
  • Ask the tenant exactly how they'll use it, including seating, equipment and expected visitors.
  • Check zoning for that use, plus parking.
  • Decide in the lease who applies for permits, who pays for plans and work, and what happens to the rent start date if approvals run long.
  • Clear any open violations on the building first.

Your attorney drafts and reviews the lease terms. The building and zoning offices are the final word on what a specific space needs, so get their answer in writing for your file.
