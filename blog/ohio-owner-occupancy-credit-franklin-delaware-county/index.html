Ohio's owner-occupancy credit: who files, when, and what changes starting in 2026

If you own the home you live in anywhere in Ohio, there's a property tax reduction you may be missing: the owner-occupancy credit. It isn't automatic. You apply for it with the county auditor, once, on a one-page state form. And starting with tax year 2026, a state law passed last year makes it worth a good deal more than it used to be, while a separate credit that rental and investment homes get is being phased out. Here's how it works in Franklin and Delaware counties, with the public source for each point.

What the credit is

The Franklin County Auditor describes the owner-occupancy credit as the program formerly known as the 2.5% supplemental rollback. [1] The state's application form says it cuts the taxes charged by qualified levies by 2.5%. [2] "Qualified levies" is doing real work in that sentence. The credit applies only to tax levies passed during or before the November 2013 general election, so newer levies on your bill aren't reduced by it. [1]

Who qualifies

Two conditions matter most. You have to own and live in the home as your principal place of residence on January 1 of the year you file. [2] And a homeowner and spouse can claim it on one home only, unless they can show they live in separate homes. [2] The Franklin County Auditor states the same rule: own and occupy the home as your primary domicile as of January 1 of the tax year, on one property only. [1]

That January 1 date is the part buyers trip on. If you closed on your house in, say, March 2026 and moved in, you didn't live there on January 1, 2026. Your first filing year is 2027. If you've lived there since before January 1, 2026 and never filed, you can file this year.

How and when to apply

The form is DTE 105C, Application for Owner-Occupancy Tax Reduction, from the Ohio Department of Taxation. [2] Franklin County posts a copy on its website. [3] You file it with the auditor of the county where the home sits, so a home on the Delaware County side goes to the Delaware County Auditor. The form says to file no later than December 31. [2] It also has a box for a late application: if you qualified last year but didn't file, you can apply for the prior year too. [2]

The form asks for the parcel number, the date you acquired the home, whether the lot is more than one acre, and whether you own a second or vacation home. [2] You sign it under penalty of perjury, so answer the second-home question carefully. [2]

Not sure whether your parcel already has the credit? Look up the parcel on the county auditor's site, or call the auditor's office.

What changes starting with tax year 2026

House Bill 186, signed in December 2025, changes how these credits work. [4] The Legislative Service Commission's final analysis says the act took effect March 20, 2026, and the changes begin to apply in tax year 2026. [5]

Today an owner-occupied home gets two reductions on those older levies: a 10% rollback that most residential property gets, plus the 2.5% owner-occupancy credit, for 12.5% in all. HB 186 raises that total for owner-occupied homes to 15.38%. [5] It does it in two moves at once. The 10% rollback steps down to 7.5% in tax year 2026, 5% in 2027, 2.5% in 2028 and zero in 2029, while the owner-occupied rollback rises from 5.70% in 2026 to 15.38% in 2029 (the analysis lists 8.92 for 2027 and 12.15 for 2028). [5] Add the two together and an owner-occupied home's combined reduction goes up a little each year.

The Ohio House's announcement says homeowners will begin to see the larger credit in January 2027. [4] Tax year 2026 bills are paid in 2027, which is likely why the dates differ; your county auditor can confirm how it shows up on your bill.

If you own a rental

This is where the law cuts the other way. The LSC analysis says HB 186 eliminates the rollback for residential property that is leased to renters or otherwise not used as the owner's primary residence. [5] That's the 10% rollback stepping down to zero by 2029. [5] Agricultural land other than timberland keeps the full 10%. [5]

So if you own a single-family rental or a small multifamily building in Franklin or Delaware County, plan on a higher net tax bill each year through 2029, all else equal. It's worth building into your rent and budget planning now. And if a home you lived in becomes a rental, the owner-occupancy credit no longer fits it, since the home has to be your principal residence. [2] Tell the auditor rather than wait for the county to find it.

A short checklist

  • Living in a home you own on January 1? File DTE 105C with that county's auditor by December 31.
  • Bought this year? Mark January 2027 on your calendar and file then.
  • Missed last year? Use the late-application box on the same form.
  • Own a second home or a rental? Claim the credit on your principal residence only.
  • Landlord? Expect the 10% rollback to shrink each year through 2029.

If you're 65 or older or have a disability, ask the auditor about the separate homestead exemption too. It has its own form and rules.

Your county auditor is the final word on your parcel, and a tax advisor can tell you how this affects your own bill. This post is about the credit itself.
