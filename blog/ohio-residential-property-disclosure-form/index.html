The Ohio Residential Property Disclosure Form: what sellers fill out and when buyers can back out

If you're selling a house in Westerville, Columbus or anywhere else in Ohio, you'll probably be asked to fill out the state's Residential Property Disclosure Form. It's six pages, and the timing of when you hand it over matters more than most sellers expect. Here is what the form asks, who has to use it, and how it connects to a buyer's right to cancel.

Where the requirement comes from

The rule is in Ohio Revised Code 5302.30. It covers residential property with a building that has one to four dwelling units, so a single-family house, a condo, a duplex or a fourplex. It applies to sales and to a few other kinds of transfers, including land installment contracts, leases with an option to buy and exchanges. [1]

The form itself is written by the Ohio Department of Commerce. Ohio Administrative Code 1301:5-6-10 adopts it, and the current version took effect June 1, 2022. [3] You can download it free from the Division of Real Estate and Professional Licensing. [2] If someone hands you an older version, ask for the 2022 form instead.

Who doesn't have to fill it out

The statute lists transfers that are exempt. Some of the ones sellers ask about most: [1]

  • Sales ordered by a court, including probate sales, foreclosure sales and sales by a bankruptcy trustee.
  • A sale by a fiduciary handling an estate, guardianship, conservatorship or trust.
  • A transfer from one co-owner to the other co-owners.
  • A transfer to the seller's spouse or children and other lineal descendants, or between spouses under a divorce or dissolution.
  • Newly built homes that nobody has lived in yet.
  • A sale to a buyer who has lived in the home as their residence for at least a year right before the sale, like a tenant buying the house they rent.
  • A sale by an owner who inherited the property and hasn't lived in it within the past year.

That last one comes up a lot with inherited houses. Even when the form isn't required, buyers' agents will often ask for whatever information you do have.

What the form asks about

The 2022 form walks through these sections in order: [2]

  • Water supply
  • Sewer system
  • Roof
  • Water intrusion
  • Structural components, such as the foundation, basement or crawl space, floors and walls, plus any fire or smoke damage
  • Wood destroying insects and termites
  • Mechanical systems, from electrical, plumbing, heating and air to the sump pump, fireplace and built-in appliances
  • Hazardous materials, including lead-based paint, asbestos, urea-formaldehyde foam insulation and radon
  • Underground storage tanks and wells, including oil, gas and mineral rights leases
  • Flood plain and Lake Erie coastal erosion area
  • Drainage and erosion
  • Zoning, code violations, assessments and homeowners' association, including historic designation
  • Boundary lines, encroachments, shared driveways and party walls
  • Other known material defects

Several sections ask about repairs and problems "but not longer than the past 5 years," including the water supply, sewer, roof, structural, termite, mechanical and drainage questions. The water intrusion section doesn't carry that limit. [2] If you're not sure whether something counts, say what you know. Disclosing a past leak that was fixed, with the date and who fixed it, usually goes over better with buyers than an unexplained "unknown."

You answer from what you actually know

The statute says the form covers material defects within the seller's actual knowledge. It also says the form is not a warranty of any kind and is not a substitute for an inspection, and it encourages buyers to get a professional inspection. [1] The form repeats that in capital letters. [2]

In practice that means you don't need to hire anyone to fill it out, and you shouldn't guess. Answer what you know from owning and living in the house. If you have receipts, warranties or reports for a roof, furnace or basement repair, pull them together now. They help you answer accurately, and buyers like seeing them.

Timing and the buyer's right to cancel

The seller has to give the signed form to each prospective buyer or the buyer's agent "as soon as is practicable." [1] The reason to get it out early is the rescission rule in division (K) of the statute.

If a buyer gets the form, or an amendment to it, after they've already signed a purchase agreement, they can cancel by delivering a written, signed and dated rescission to the seller or the seller's agent. They have to do it within three business days after they or their agent receive the form. The right also ends at the earlier of the closing or 30 days after the seller accepted the offer. If the buyer cancels, their deposit is returned. [1] [2]

If the buyer received the form before making the offer and signing the agreement, that rescission right doesn't apply. [1] So the simplest way to avoid a surprise cancellation is to have the completed form ready when the listing goes live, so buyers' agents can share it before their clients write an offer.

One more detail: if you learn about a new problem after you've filled out the form, the form can be amended, and an amendment delivered after the contract is signed starts that three-business-day window again. [1] [2]

Notices for buyers on the form

The form also carries two notices aimed at buyers. One says buyers should check on Ohio's sex offender registration and notification law through the local sheriff's office. The other says buyers should check for abandoned underground mines with the Ohio Department of Natural Resources. [2] Sellers don't fill anything in for these. They tell the buyer where to look.

Homes built before 1978 also need a separate federal lead-based paint disclosure, which is not part of the Ohio form. [4]

A short checklist before you list

  • Download the current form (effective June 2022) and fill it out from your own knowledge.
  • Gather receipts and reports for any repairs from roughly the last five years.
  • Note anything about the sewer or septic system, sump pump and drainage.
  • If the home was built before 1978, have the federal lead paint disclosure ready too.
  • Have the signed form ready to share before offers come in.
