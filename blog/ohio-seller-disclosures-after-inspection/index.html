Ohio Seller Disclosures: What to Know After a Home Inspection

By Travis Debnam | February 2026 | 7 min read

When you get a pre-listing inspection, the report gives you detailed information about your home's condition. That information matters when it comes to filling out Ohio's seller disclosure form. Here is a general overview of how inspection findings and disclosure requirements relate to each other.

Disclaimer: This article provides general information about Ohio seller disclosures and is not legal advice. Every situation is different. Consult a licensed Ohio real estate attorney for guidance specific to your circumstances.

Ohio Residential Property Disclosure Form: An Overview

Ohio Revised Code Section 5302.30 requires sellers of residential property to complete and provide a Residential Property Disclosure Form to prospective buyers. The form covers the seller's knowledge of the property's condition across multiple categories, including:

  • Water supply and sewage systems
  • Roof condition and age
  • Basement and foundation (including history of water intrusion)
  • Heating and cooling systems
  • Electrical system
  • Plumbing
  • Presence of hazardous materials (lead paint, asbestos, radon)
  • Structural issues
  • Drainage and flooding history
  • Zoning violations or non-conforming uses
  • Boundary or encroachment issues

The form asks sellers to indicate whether they are aware of any defects or issues in each category. Sellers answer based on their actual knowledge — the form is not asking sellers to hire professionals or investigate hidden conditions.

What Sellers Must Disclose: Known Material Defects

Ohio law requires sellers to disclose known material defects. A material defect is generally understood as a condition that would affect a reasonable buyer's decision to purchase the property or the price they would offer. This includes conditions that affect:

  • The structural integrity of the home
  • The function of major systems (HVAC, plumbing, electrical)
  • Health and safety (mold, radon, lead paint)
  • Water intrusion or drainage problems
  • The legal use of the property (zoning, permits)

The key word is "known." If you are not aware of a condition, you are not expected to disclose it. However, once you become aware — for example, through a home inspection — that condition is now part of your knowledge.

How Inspection Findings Affect Disclosure

This is the practical intersection that matters for sellers considering a pre-listing inspection. When an inspector identifies an issue, you now have documented knowledge of that condition. Common examples:

  • Basement moisture: If the inspection report notes evidence of water intrusion in the basement, that becomes a known condition for the disclosure form, even if you were not previously aware of it.
  • Aging roof: If the inspector documents that the roof is nearing end of life, this is information that should be reflected in the disclosure.
  • Electrical concerns: If the report identifies an outdated panel or wiring issues, these are now known conditions.
  • Radon: If radon testing shows levels above the EPA action level of 4 pCi/L, this is information buyers are entitled to know.

Some sellers worry that a pre-listing inspection creates disclosure obligations they would rather avoid. In practice, the opposite approach — not knowing about problems — often leads to worse outcomes. When a buyer's inspection discovers issues that the seller should have reasonably known about, it can undermine trust and create legal exposure.

Known Defects vs. Normal Wear and Tear

Not every inspection finding is a material defect. Homes age, and normal wear is expected. The distinction matters:

  • Material defect: A furnace with a cracked heat exchanger (safety concern), foundation wall with active structural movement, or a roof with active leaks.
  • Normal wear: Minor cosmetic cracks in drywall, some weathering on exterior paint, a water heater that is functional but older.

The disclosure form asks about conditions that are defective or malfunctioning, not about age or cosmetic condition. A 15-year-old furnace that works properly does not require the same disclosure as one that has been red-tagged by a technician.

Your agent can help you distinguish between items that need to be disclosed and items that are normal for a home of your property's age and type. For questions about specific conditions, an attorney is the appropriate resource.

When to Consult an Attorney

Consider consulting an Ohio real estate attorney when:

  • The inspection reveals a significant structural, environmental, or safety issue
  • You are unsure whether a specific finding qualifies as a material defect
  • There are past repairs (such as foundation work or mold remediation) that you need to determine how to disclose
  • You received a pre-listing inspection report and want guidance on updating your disclosure form
  • You have any concern about potential liability

An attorney can review your specific situation and advise you on how to complete the disclosure form accurately and completely.

Why Pre-Listing Inspections Help With Disclosures

A pre-listing inspection does create documentation — and that is a good thing. Here is why:

  1. You can address issues before listing. If the inspection finds a problem, you can fix it. A repaired issue is disclosed differently than an unresolved one.
  2. Your disclosure is more accurate. A thorough understanding of your home's condition leads to a more complete disclosure form, which protects you.
  3. Buyers have more confidence. When sellers provide an inspection report and a well-completed disclosure upfront, it signals transparency. Transactions with transparent sellers tend to close more smoothly.
  4. You reduce post-closing risk. The most common disclosure disputes happen when buyers discover issues the seller knew about (or should have known about) but did not disclose. A pre-listing inspection and honest disclosure reduce this risk.
