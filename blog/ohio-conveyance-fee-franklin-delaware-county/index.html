Ohio conveyance fees in Franklin and Delaware counties: what sellers pay at closing

Every Ohio property sale carries a conveyance fee, paid to the county auditor when the deed is transferred. Here is how it is figured in Franklin and Delaware counties, who pays it, and when a transfer is exempt.

What the conveyance fee is

Ohio's real property conveyance fee is a tax on the value of real estate sold or transferred from one person to another. It has two parts. The state charges a mandatory 1 mill, which is $1 for every $1,000 of value. Each county may add a permissive fee of up to 3 more mills, so the total in any county runs from $1 to $4 per $1,000. The authority for it sits in Ohio Revised Code sections 319.54 and 322.01 to 322.07. [1]

The fee is paid by the seller, not the buyer. The Ohio Department of Taxation describes it as paid by persons who make sales of real estate, generally as part of the closing process. [1] The County Commissioners Association of Ohio puts it the same way: the fee is paid by the transferor, not the recipient. [2] In practice your title company collects it from the seller's side of the settlement statement and pays the auditor when the deed is recorded.

Franklin County: $3 per $1,000

Franklin County's rate is $3 per $1,000 of the sale price. That is the state's $1 plus a $2 county fee set by the Franklin County Board of Commissioners, which last changed it in August 2019. [3]

A few details from the Franklin County Auditor's calculator matter at closing: [3]

  • The sale price is rounded up to the nearest $100 before the fee is figured.
  • There is a $3 minimum conveyance fee and a $0.50 transfer tax on every non-exempt transfer.
  • If the seller receives the homestead exemption on the property, the fee drops to a total of $1 per $1,000.
  • An exempt transfer still owes the $0.50 transfer tax.

Delaware County: also $3 per $1,000

Delaware County also charges a $2 permissive fee on top of the state's $1, for a total of $3 per $1,000. [2] The County Commissioners Association's June 2026 report, built from Ohio Department of Taxation data for calendar year 2024, counts 4,666 conveyances in Delaware County that year. [2] The Delaware County Auditor's office handles the transfer and can confirm its current per-parcel charges before you close.

Neighboring counties for comparison

The same report lists Licking County at a $2 permissive fee ($3 total) and Union and Fairfield counties at the $3 maximum ($4 total). Statewide, 62 counties charge the maximum. [2] If you own property in more than one central Ohio county, the rate can differ by a dollar per $1,000 from one closing to the next.

Worked examples

These use the rates above. Your title company's settlement statement is the final word. [3]

  • A $350,000 house in Franklin County. $350,000 divided by 1,000 is 350. At $3, the conveyance fee is $1,050, plus the $0.50 transfer tax.
  • The same house, seller receiving the homestead exemption. At $1 per $1,000, the fee is $350, plus $0.50.
  • A $1,200,000 commercial building in Franklin County. 1,200 times $3 is $3,600, plus $0.50.
  • A $500,000 property in Delaware County. At $3 per $1,000, the fee is $1,500, plus any transfer charge the Delaware County Auditor applies. [2]

The fee is based on the price on the conveyance form, which is not necessarily the auditor's appraised value. [2] A property that sells above its appraised value pays on the sale price.

The forms: DTE 100 and DTE 100EX

A taxable sale is reported on form DTE 100. A transfer that qualifies for an exemption uses form DTE 100EX instead, and the seller marks the lettered exemption that applies. [4] The Franklin County Auditor points out that only certain listed relationships and situations qualify, and that a transfer where money changes hands is taxable. A transfer with no money can still be taxable as a gift. [4]

Common exemptions

Ohio law lists the exemptions in Revised Code sections 319.202 and 319.54(G)(3). The Franklin County Auditor's exemption list includes, among others: [5]

  • A gift between husband and wife, or between parent and child or the spouse of either.
  • A transfer to heirs or devisees when no money is paid.
  • A transfer to or from a person when no money or other valuable consideration is paid, with an affidavit of facts.
  • Transfers between spouses, survivorship transfers, and certain transfers into and out of trusts.
  • Leases, and transfers where the value does not exceed $100.
  • Transfers to 501(c)(3) organizations without consideration.

An exemption is a legal question about your specific transfer. Your title company or attorney should confirm which one, if any, applies.

What this means for a seller's net

The conveyance fee is one line among several on a seller's settlement statement, alongside the payoff of any mortgage, prorated property taxes, title charges and brokerage fees. Because it scales with price, it is easy to estimate early: multiply the expected price by the county's rate per $1,000, then add the transfer tax.
