Sell and Buy at the Same Time: Timing and Contingency Options

By Travis Debnam | February 2026 | 8 min read

Most homeowners who are ready to move face the same question: do you sell your current home first, buy the next one first, or try to do both at once? Each approach has tradeoffs. Here is how the common options work in the Central Ohio market.

Option 1: Sell First, Then Buy

You list and sell your current home, close the transaction, and then begin searching for your next home.

Advantages

  • You know your exact budget. With sale proceeds in hand, you know precisely how much you have for a down payment and can make a clean, non-contingent offer on your next home.
  • Stronger buying position. Sellers prefer offers from buyers who are not dependent on another transaction closing. Your offer competes better in multiple-offer situations.
  • No risk of carrying two mortgages. You eliminate the financial stress of paying for two homes simultaneously.

Disadvantages

  • You need somewhere to live in between. Unless your closing dates align with a new purchase (which is difficult to guarantee), you may need temporary housing — staying with family, a short-term rental, or a month-to-month lease.
  • Two moves. Moving once to temporary housing and again to your new home is inconvenient and adds cost.
  • Market risk. If home prices increase between selling and buying, you may pay more for your next home than you anticipated.

This approach works well for sellers who have flexible living arrangements or who are moving to a less competitive market where homes are available. It is common for sellers in Westerville and Dublin who plan to move to areas with more inventory.

Option 2: Buy First, Then Sell

You purchase your next home before listing your current one.

Advantages

  • No temporary housing needed. You move directly from your current home to the new one.
  • You can take your time preparing your current home for sale. An empty, staged home often shows better and may sell faster.
  • One move. You only move once, which is simpler and less expensive.

Disadvantages

  • Carrying two mortgages. Until your current home sells, you are responsible for both mortgage payments, property taxes, insurance, and maintenance on two homes.
  • Qualifying for two loans. Your lender needs to approve you for the new mortgage while your current mortgage is still outstanding. This can be challenging depending on your debt-to-income ratio.
  • Pressure to sell. If your current home takes longer to sell than expected, the financial burden increases.

This approach works best for homeowners who have significant equity, strong income relative to their debt, or the ability to qualify for both mortgages comfortably.

Option 3: Simultaneous Close With Contingencies

You list your current home and make an offer on a new home at the same time, using contingencies to connect the two transactions.

How It Works

Your offer on the new home includes a home sale contingency, meaning the purchase is contingent on your current home selling and closing. Both transactions are scheduled to close on the same day or within a few days of each other.

Advantages

  • One move. Ideally, you close on both properties the same day and move directly.
  • No period carrying two mortgages. The proceeds from your sale fund your purchase.
  • No temporary housing. The timeline is designed to avoid a gap.

Disadvantages

  • Your offer is weaker. A home sale contingency makes your offer less attractive to sellers, especially in competitive markets like Powell or Columbus.
  • Coordination complexity. Two transactions need to close within the same narrow window. If one is delayed (appraisal issues, lender delays, inspection negotiations), both are affected.
  • Risk of losing the new home. If the seller of the home you want to buy receives a non-contingent offer, they may invoke a "kick-out" clause that gives you a short window to remove your contingency or lose the home.

This approach requires careful coordination and a good agent who can manage both timelines. It works best when your current home is in a strong position to sell quickly — priced right, in good condition, and in a desirable area.

Option 4: Bridge Strategies

When a clean sequence is not possible, several bridge strategies can fill the gap.

Rent-Back Agreement

You sell your current home but negotiate a rent-back period (typically 30 to 60 days) where you remain in the home as a tenant after closing. You pay the buyer a daily rental rate while you finalize your next purchase.

Pros: One move, you receive sale proceeds to use toward the new purchase, and the buyer gets their home (just with a short delay on possession).

Cons: Not all buyers agree to rent-back arrangements. Some loan programs limit rent-back periods. You are living in a home you no longer own, which requires clear terms for maintenance, insurance, and security deposit.

Temporary Housing

You sell, move to a short-term rental or stay with family, and then buy. Common options include month-to-month apartment leases, furnished short-term rentals, or extended-stay accommodations.

Pros: Full financial flexibility — you have sale proceeds and no pressure to buy on someone else's timeline.

Cons: Two moves, storage costs for furniture, and the inconvenience of a temporary living situation.

Bridge Loans

A bridge loan is a short-term loan that uses your current home's equity to fund the down payment on your new home. You repay the bridge loan when your current home sells.

Pros: Lets you buy before selling without a home sale contingency, making your offer stronger.

Cons: Higher interest rates than a standard mortgage, additional closing costs, and the risk of carrying the loan longer than expected if your home takes time to sell. Not all lenders offer bridge loans.

How a Pre-Listing Inspection Helps With Timing

Regardless of which approach you choose, a pre-listing inspection reduces timing risk on the sell side. When you know your home's condition before listing, you can:

  • Price accurately from day one, which leads to faster offers
  • Address issues before listing instead of during the buyer's due diligence period
  • Reduce the chance of delays from inspection negotiations
  • Provide the inspection report to buyers, which can shorten or eliminate the buyer's inspection contingency period

A faster, smoother sale on your current home makes every approach above easier to execute.

Choosing the Right Approach

The right strategy depends on your financial situation, timeline, and risk tolerance. Consider these factors:

  • How quickly will your current home sell? In desirable Central Ohio areas, well-priced homes often sell within 2 to 4 weeks. That fast timeline opens up options.
  • Can you qualify for two mortgages? Talk to your lender early. Understanding your borrowing capacity shapes which options are available.
  • Do you have temporary housing options? Family nearby, a flexible lease, or comfort with short-term rentals can make the sell-first approach painless.
  • How competitive is the market where you are buying? If your target area has multiple-offer situations, a contingent offer may not be competitive. Selling first or using a bridge loan strengthens your position.
