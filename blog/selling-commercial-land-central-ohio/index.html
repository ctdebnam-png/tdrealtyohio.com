Selling commercial land in central Ohio: what to sort out before you list

Vacant commercial land sells on what a buyer can build there, how fast, and what it will cost to get started. Buyers and their lenders will dig into zoning, taxes, title, survey and flood status before they close. If you're selling a lot or acreage in Columbus, Westerville or elsewhere in Franklin and Delaware counties, the sooner you know those answers yourself, the fewer surprises you'll hit after an offer comes in. Here is what to check, with the public sources for each.

1. Know what the zoning allows

Zoning decides what a buyer can legally put on the land, so it shapes who your buyers are. A parcel zoned for general commercial use draws a different crowd than one zoned for light industrial or offices. Start by confirming which government zones the parcel. A mailing address that says Columbus or Westerville doesn't settle it, because many addresses with those city names sit in a township. The county auditor's parcel record shows the taxing district.

Columbus and Westerville both post free zoning maps. Columbus is also moving parcels to a new zoning code in phases, so check which code applies before you read what a district allows. We walked through both cities step by step in How to look up zoning on a Columbus or Westerville property. If a likely buyer would need a rezoning or variance, say so up front. It affects the timeline they'll ask for in the contract.

2. Check whether the land is taxed as farmland

A lot of the larger undeveloped tracts around central Ohio are still enrolled in the Current Agricultural Use Value program, called CAUV. Land in the program is taxed on its farm value instead of its market value. That matters for a sale because of what happens when the land stops being farmed.

Under Ohio Revised Code 5713.34, when land devoted exclusively to agricultural use is converted, the county auditor levies a recoupment charge equal to the tax reduction the land received during the three tax years immediately before the year of conversion. That charge is a lien on the converted land. If only part of a parcel converts, the auditor splits the parcel and charges only the converted part. [1]

Look at your property tax bill or the auditor's parcel page to see whether CAUV applies. If it does, decide early how the purchase contract will handle the recoupment charge, because a commercial buyer is going to convert the land. Who pays it is negotiable, and your attorney or title company can help you word it.

3. Order the title work early

A title commitment shows who holds title and what's recorded against the land: easements, mortgages, liens, deed restrictions and access rights. On vacant land the easements and restrictions get the most attention, since a utility easement across the middle of a lot or an old restriction on use can limit what a buyer can build. Pulling a commitment before you list lets you find and clear problems on your own schedule instead of during the buyer's inspection period.

4. Expect a survey request

Commercial buyers and their lenders usually want an ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey. That's a survey prepared to national standards set jointly by the American Land Title Association and the National Society of Professional Surveyors, so a title company can insure against boundary, access and encroachment issues. The current standards took effect February 23, 2026. [2] [3]

If you already have a survey, find it. Even an older one helps a buyer's surveyor and can answer early questions about boundaries and access. Who orders and pays for a new survey is another item the purchase contract settles.

5. Look up the flood zone

Flood status changes what a buyer can build and what their lender will require. FEMA's Flood Map Service Center lets anyone search an address and view or download the official flood map for free. [4] If part of the land sits in a mapped flood hazard area, note it in the listing. Buyers will find it anyway, and it reads better coming from you.

6. Gather what you know about utilities and access

Builders want to know whether water, sanitary sewer, gas and electric reach the property line, and where the nearest connections are. They'll also ask about road frontage and curb cuts. You don't need engineering studies to list, but any letters from utility providers, past site plans, or approvals from earlier proposals are worth pulling together. Information like this saves a buyer weeks of calls, and it can make your land the easier choice.

7. The residential disclosure form doesn't apply, but honesty still does

Ohio's Residential Property Disclosure Form is required for property with one to four dwelling units. [5] Vacant commercial land isn't covered. That doesn't mean you can stay quiet about problems you know about. Commercial buyers do their own due diligence, and the purchase contract will usually include representations about the property. Answer what you know accurately and let the buyer verify the rest. For homes, see our post on the Ohio Residential Property Disclosure Form.

8. Plan for the conveyance fee

When the deed transfers, Ohio charges a conveyance fee, and in practice the seller usually pays it at closing through the title company. Franklin and Delaware counties both charge $3 per $1,000 of the sale price. The math, forms and exemptions are in our conveyance fee post.

A short checklist before you list

  • Confirm the zoning jurisdiction and district, and whether a buyer's likely use would need a rezoning or variance.
  • Check whether the land is in CAUV and plan for the recoupment charge.
  • Order a title commitment and read the easements and restrictions.
  • Find any existing survey; expect the buyer's lender to want an ALTA/NSPS survey.
  • Look up the FEMA flood map for the parcel.
  • Gather utility letters, past site plans and anything else on access and services.
