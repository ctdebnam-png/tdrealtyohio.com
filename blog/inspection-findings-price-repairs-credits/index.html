How Inspection Findings Affect Price, Repairs, and Credits

By Travis Debnam | February 2026 | 7 min read

Every home inspection turns up findings. What matters is how you handle them. Here is how inspection results typically get resolved in Central Ohio real estate transactions — and why addressing issues before listing gives sellers an advantage.

The Three Common Responses to Inspection Findings

When a buyer's inspection reveals issues, the negotiation typically follows one of three paths. Each has tradeoffs for both the seller and the buyer.

1. Seller Makes Repairs Before Closing

The seller hires a contractor to complete the repair before the closing date. The buyer may request a re-inspection to confirm the work was done properly.

When this works well: Safety issues, code violations, and items that could affect the buyer's financing or insurance. Lenders may require certain repairs (such as a non-functional furnace or active roof leak) before approving the loan. In these cases, the seller completing the repair is often the most straightforward path to closing.

Potential downsides: The seller is choosing the contractor and managing the timeline, which can create tension if the buyer disagrees with the quality of work. Repairs also need to be completed within the contract timeline, which can be tight.

2. Seller Offers a Credit at Closing

Instead of making the repair, the seller provides a dollar amount as a credit to the buyer at closing. The buyer then handles the repair after they take ownership.

When this works well: Items where the buyer may want to choose their own contractor, select specific materials, or bundle the repair with other planned renovations. For example, if the inspection notes an aging but functional furnace, a credit lets the buyer replace it on their own timeline and choose their preferred equipment.

Potential downsides: There are limits on how much credit a lender will allow (often capped at a percentage of the purchase price). Credits also need to be documented on the closing statement, and some loan programs have restrictions on seller concessions.

3. Purchase Price Is Adjusted

The buyer and seller agree to lower the purchase price to account for the cost of repairs. The buyer handles the work after closing.

When this works well: Larger issues where a credit would exceed lender limits, or when the buyer wants maximum flexibility. A price reduction also lowers the buyer's property taxes going forward since the assessed value may reflect the lower sale price.

Potential downsides: A lower sale price means lower proceeds for the seller. It can also affect the appraisal if the adjusted price falls below comparable sales. This approach is most common for significant findings, not minor items.

Common Inspection Findings in Central Ohio

The age and style of homes varies across Central Ohio, but certain findings come up regularly in this market.

Aging Furnaces

Many homes in Westerville and Columbus neighborhoods built in the 1980s and 1990s have furnaces approaching or past their expected lifespan of 15 to 20 years. An old furnace that is still functional may not require immediate replacement, but buyers often ask for a credit toward a new unit.

Basement Moisture

Basement moisture is one of the most common findings in Central Ohio homes. Signs include efflorescence on block walls, staining on floors, and musty odors. The response depends on severity: simple grading corrections and downspout extensions may solve minor moisture issues, while persistent water intrusion may require interior or exterior waterproofing.

Aging Roofs

Asphalt shingle roofs in Central Ohio typically last 20 to 25 years, depending on installation quality and weather exposure. Homes in Dublin and Powell built in the late 1990s or early 2000s may have roofs nearing the end of their useful life. If a roof has several years of life remaining but is aging, a credit toward future replacement is a common resolution.

Electrical Panel Issues

Older homes may have outdated electrical panels (such as Federal Pacific or Zinsco brands) that are commonly flagged by inspectors due to known reliability concerns. Replacement is typically recommended and can be a significant negotiation point.

Why a Pre-Listing Inspection Changes the Dynamic

When a seller orders a pre-listing inspection, the negotiation dynamic shifts. Instead of reacting to a buyer's inspection report under contract pressure, the seller can:

  • Choose which repairs to make before listing, on their own timeline and with contractors they trust
  • Price the home accurately based on its actual condition, reducing the chance of price reductions later
  • Provide the inspection report to buyers upfront, building confidence and reducing the chance of a second inspection revealing surprises
  • Avoid deal-killing surprises that cause buyers to walk away during the contingency period

Deciding What to Fix vs. What to Disclose

Not every inspection finding needs to be repaired before listing. A practical approach:

  1. Fix safety issues and code violations. These are non-negotiable. Items like missing GFCI outlets, non-functional smoke detectors, or gas leaks should be addressed immediately.
  2. Fix items that affect financing. Lenders require the home to be in habitable condition. Non-functional HVAC, active leaks, and structural concerns can prevent loan approval.
  3. Evaluate cost vs. impact for everything else. If a repair costs $200 and removes a negotiation point, it is usually worth doing. If a repair costs $5,000 and the buyer may want to handle it differently, consider disclosing the condition and pricing accordingly.
